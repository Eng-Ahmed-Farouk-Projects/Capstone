01 / The challenge

When a child has a problem, who listens?

Poverty, bullying, constant comparison, limited family communication, and social pressures can undermine a young person's sense of safety and belonging. Some children hesitate to speak up because they fear blame, ridicule, rejection, or being misunderstood.

When trusted support is missing, children may withdraw, struggle to ask for help, or experience difficulties with participation and learning. These outcomes are not inevitable, but they make early support important.

Core insight: The problem is not only the adversity children face. It is also the possibility of facing it without a trusted, safe, and accessible support system.
03 / Problem statement

Defining the problem

Children and adolescents aged 6–18 may experience bullying, poverty, social comparison, and limited emotional support at home or in their communities. Without safe and trusted channels to express concerns and seek help, some may struggle with self-confidence, social participation, and learning. There is a need for accessible, supportive environments that help young people feel heard, develop social-emotional skills, and connect with appropriate protection and support services.

Our guiding question

How might we create safe and accessible environments where children feel heard, valued, and empowered to navigate social and emotional challenges?

05 / Stakeholder conversations

Understanding different perspectives

The following are illustrative interview drafts to guide stakeholder research. They are not verified quotations or evidence that the named individuals expressed these views.

Shams Mohamed — Children's Programming Instructor

Q: How can bullying affect a child's learning?

A: A child who fears being laughed at may hesitate to answer questions or try something new. Learning environments should make mistakes part of the process rather than a reason for humiliation.

Q: What could help?

A: Cooperative activities, respectful communication, anti-bullying awareness, and clear procedures for referring serious concerns to appropriate adults or specialists.

Enass Mohamed — Mother of Two

Q: Why might children feel unheard at home?

A: Family responsibilities and daily pressures can make meaningful conversations difficult. Even when parents care deeply, children may need more time, patience, and attentive listening.

Q: What could help families?

A: Practical guidance on listening without immediate judgment, discussing difficult situations calmly, and responding to children's concerns with care.

Rima Yousef — Age 9

Q: What helps a child feel safe when sharing a problem?

A: A child-friendly response might emphasize being listened to, not being laughed at, and receiving calm help instead of immediate blame.

Q: What could make school better?

A: A child might ask for less teasing and a trusted adult they can approach when something goes wrong.

Research ethics: conduct the actual interviews before attributing answers to participants. For a child participant, obtain guardian consent and the child's assent, avoid leading questions, and protect their privacy and safety.

06 / Evidence base

What research tells us

WHO — Adolescent Mental Health

Explains how poverty, violence, bullying, social exclusion, peer pressure, and family environments relate to adolescent mental health. WHO estimates that one in seven people aged 10–19 experiences a mental disorder globally.

UNICEF — Behind the Numbers: Ending School Violence and Bullying

Reviews evidence on school violence and bullying and discusses evidence-based approaches to prevention and response.

UNICEF Innocenti — Mind the Gap

Maps evidence on mental-health and psychosocial-support interventions for children and adolescents in low- and middle-income countries.

WHO — Child and Adolescent Mental and Brain Health

Explains the importance of supportive environments and the potential effects of adverse experiences, including bullying and poverty.

08 / Proposed response

From being unheard to being supported

Safe spaces

Age-appropriate activities where young people can express concerns without ridicule.

Social-emotional skills

Workshops on communication, confidence, conflict resolution, and asking for help.

Family engagement

Practical sessions that help caregivers listen and respond supportively.

Safeguarding & referral

Clear procedures for bullying, abuse disclosures, and referrals to qualified professionals.

How will success be measured?

Track changes in children's knowledge of how to seek help, perceived safety and belonging, participation, caregiver listening practices, and awareness of bullying-reporting procedures. Establish a baseline before the intervention and evaluate outcomes afterward.

Every child deserves to be heard.

A supportive environment cannot remove every hardship, but it can help children find trusted adults, access appropriate support, and develop the skills they need to navigate difficult situations.
